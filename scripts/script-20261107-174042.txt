**Narration (138 words):**
In November 1930, Canadian fur trapper Joe Labelle sought shelter from a bitter blizzard near the shores of Lake Anjikuni in Nunavut. He stumbled upon an Inuit village of roughly thirty souls. To his shock, the fires were still burning, and pots of stew sat half-cooked. Yet, the settlement was completely deserted. 

Every single resident had vanished, leaving behind their winter clothing, food supplies, and even their sled dogs—which were found starved to death, tied just outside their buried homes. Labelle immediately telegraphed the Royal Canadian Mounted Police. Officers searched the area and found footprints leading nowhere in the deep snow.

### Fact vs. Speculation Breakdown

**Verified Facts:**
* Joe Labelle was a real fur trapper who operated in northern Canada.
* In late 1930, Labelle reported finding an abandoned Inuit campsite near Lake Anjikuni and notified the Royal Canadian Mounted Police (RCMP).
* RCMP officers investigated the report and confirmed the camp was abandoned, noting that supplies and gear were left behind.

**Speculation / Urban Legend:**
* The sensationalist details—such as the village having a population of 2,000, pots of stew still warm on the fires, sled dogs starving while tied up, graves being dug up, and mysterious lights in the sky—were heavily embellished by journalist Emmett McGrath in 1931 and later popularized by paranormal writers like Frank Edwards. 
* Modern investigations by researchers (such as those by investigator Brian Dunning) have found no official RCMP records corroborating the more extreme, supernatural claims, suggesting the event was a standard seasonal migration or temporary abandonment that was later exaggerated into a ghost story.
