**Title:** The Vanishing of Lighthouse Island

**Visual Suggestion 1:** 
A moody, cinematic drone shot of a desolate, wave-lashed rocky outcrop in the North Atlantic, slowly zooming in toward an aging stone lighthouse against a grey, overcast sky.

**Narration (138 words):**
In December 1900, a routine supply ship arrived at the Flannan Isles lighthouse off the coast of Scotland. It found a chilling scene. The heavy iron entrance gate was locked. Inside, the kitchen fire was cold, and two oilskins were missing, but the third keeper’s waterproof coat remained behind. The clocks had stopped. Most disturbingly, the logbook recorded hurricane-force winds that never actually existed in the area. The three experienced keepers—James Ducat, Thomas Marshall, and Donald MacArthur—were completely gone, leaving no bodies, no notes, and no footprints. Despite extensive searches by authorities, no trace of the men was ever found. To this day, the true fate of the keepers remains officially unexplained, giving rise to decades of dark speculation, ranging from rogue waves to supernatural abduction, though the most grounded theories point to a sudden, tragic accident at sea.

### Fact vs. Speculation Breakdown

**Verified Facts:**
* In December 1900, the supply steamer *Hesperus* arrived at the Flannan Isles lighthouse and found the station abandoned.
* The entrance gate and door were secured, the kitchen fire was unlit, and two of the three keepers' waterproof coats were missing.
* The station's clocks were not running, and the final log entries described severe, unusual storm conditions.
* A thorough search yielded no trace of keepers James Ducat, Thomas Marshall, or Donald MacArthur.

**Speculation:**
* Theories suggesting the men were swept away by a sudden rogue wave while securing equipment (the most widely accepted hypothesis, though unproven due to lack of eyewitnesses or bodies).
* Fringe and supernatural hypotheses, including abduction by pirates, foreign agents, or sea monsters (popularized by folklore and poetry, such as Wilfrid Gibson's 1912 poem *Flannan Isle*).
